Each commitment is recomputed here from its identifier and opening value, and the chain from the genesis hash — so the register is checked rather than read. The signatures are verified against the keys published in the election configuration, not against anything in the data.

Those first two are separate questions and either can fail alone. A commitment that does not recompute means the office's own opening value does not cover the identifier it published beside it — the rows below name which positions. A chain that does not reach the signed head can happen with every commitment recomputing perfectly: an entry inserted, dropped or reordered leaves each one correct and the sequence wrong. The identifiers are in doubt in the first case and the order in the second, and one verdict for both would name only the weaker finding.

Releases

Compare these identifiers against the electoral roll from the citizen registry, with current eligibility. Not against any list this office supplied: that would be comparing the office against its own copy.

Each release carries the citizen's own signature over their token request — the one object in the exchange this office could not have manufactured. A release with no signature behind it was not asked for. Verify the signatures against certificates from the certification authority, for the same reason as the roll.

What this cannot show you

Whether a token that was released was ever used. The blinding is what makes that unknowable: nothing here, and nothing in the ballot box, joins a citizen to a ballot. Absence from this register is conclusive evidence that somebody did not take part; presence is not evidence that they did.

Nor whether an office colluded with a citizen — a signed request made by a voter who then handed the token on looks exactly like any other. That is the case for splitting the issuing power across mutually independent offices, which supervision does not replace.
