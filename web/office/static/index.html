Voter Registration Office — back office

The office looking at itself. Everything below is recorded by the office in the ordinary course; none of it is disclosed to anyone.

Exposed here · X2 No deployment exposes this The office's log names who asked for a token and when. That is a participation list — the object §3.7 spends a page keeping out of anyone else's hands, because the absence of an entry is conclusive evidence that a citizen did not vote, which is exactly what an enforcer of a boycott needs. It is on screen here so the difference between what the office knows and what it says can be seen at once, and nowhere else.
Absent · A1 One office issues every token The design requires this power to be split across mutually distrustful bodies, because a single office — corrupt, breached, or merely mistaken — can mint valid-looking tokens for citizens who did not ask, and nothing in the published artefacts distinguishes them from genuine ones. The register below records the citizen's own signed request for every release, which is what lets a supervisor find a fabricated one; distributing the issuing power is what would prevent it. This demo does not.
Substituted · S2 The certificate lookup is a fixture On the wallet route the office asks driver/population_register.py for a citizen's certificate and gets one row of a table. In a deployment that is the real trust infrastructure — a published trust list and a real revocation check. On the qualified-signature route there is no lookup at all: the certificate arrives with the signature, and what is substituted there is the trust anchors instead (S6).

Qualified-signature route · what this office will believe

The second route to a token request: a citizen signs the request as a document in their own wallet and hands it in at /upload, and the office reads who they are from the signing certificate. So the whole of what it is willing to believe is below. It is published rather than kept because an office whose anchors nobody can read is an office nobody can check — which is the same oversight a deployment would get from a signed European trusted list, done by hand.

Signing · at once, or on an operator's act

A deployment has no such switch: it signs as soon as it has validated, and the reservation exists for concurrency rather than for deliberation. Checking the eligibility of an identifier and recording its release are two steps, and without an atomic claim between them two concurrent requests for one citizen can both pass — producing two tokens, with nothing in the published artefacts to show it. The switch holds that real state open long enough to be looked at.

reserved blinded value

Releasing writes the commitment and then signs, in that order and not one moment earlier: a commitment written at validation would make the pause visible to anyone counting and overstate the tokens that exist. Cancelling clears the claim, which a citizen otherwise carries for ever with nothing published and no token.

Recorded, and disclosed

§3.2: the two failures that arise before the office knows who it is speaking to must be reported identically. Otherwise anyone able to spell an identifier could submit an unsigned request and learn from the answer whether that citizen holds a certificate and sits on the register — the token endpoint would become a register lookup service, which nothing in the design intends and to which no citizen consented. Rows where the two columns differ are the boundary doing its work.

Token-release register

Published as tokens are released, as commitments rather than identifiers. The count lets anyone check that accepted ballots do not outnumber the tokens issued; the commitments let one voter, and only that voter, establish whether a token was released in their name. A plaintext register would have to surrender one of those.
